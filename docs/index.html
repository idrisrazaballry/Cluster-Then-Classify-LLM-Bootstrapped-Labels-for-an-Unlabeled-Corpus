Cluster Then Classify

Nobody labelled this training data. 7,591 news stories were clustered, Gemini was shown each cluster's top terms and asked to name it — without being told what the taxonomy was — and those names became the training labels.

On the left is the classifier that came out. On the right is the same model trained on the real labels: the ceiling you would pay an annotator to reach. Try to make them disagree.

What to notice

Business is the weak class. Recall 0.36 against precision 0.87 — it finds barely a third of Business stories, but is usually right when it does. The cause sits upstream of the classifier: k-means split Business across two clusters, and 760 of those documents landed in the cluster Gemini named World Politics. No labelling strategy recovers a distinction the clustering never drew.

The seductive number. This classifier scores 0.955 against its own held-out split while being 0.716 accurate against ground truth. That 95.5% is fidelity to k-means, not accuracy — it measures how faithfully the model reproduces the clustering, mistakes included. It is the number most likely to be quoted and the one that means least.

What the LLM actually bought. Swapping Gemini's labels for deterministic top-term strings changes accuracy by exactly zero, because renaming a cluster moves no documents. The LLM contributes readable class names derived without ground truth. The 81% of ceiling comes from the clustering.

Ablation

Everything above runs in your browser — the model is 40,776 TF-IDF features and a logistic regression, evaluated in JavaScript. No server, no inference API. The language model was called once, at training time, for a fifth of a cent.
